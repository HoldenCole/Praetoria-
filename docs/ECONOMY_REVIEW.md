# The economy, sampled against the real world

What the owner said (2026-11-22): *"I think we probably need to retune the whole economy. The best way for me to
decide that is for you to give me a sampling of how much things cost and how much you've earned from things... I think
every post, even small ones, [should pay]. The big ones should pay a salary. And you can decide to pay more or less per
person depending on how loyal they are or how good they are. They may demand more money if they are very good. But the
main thing [is] how much things cost and how resources are earned... The best way to look at it is from a real-world
perspective: return on invested capital, payback periods, salaries versus how much it costs to buy somewhere to live,
and actual ratios of wages."*

This page is that sampling. Nothing in the game was changed to write it.

## How to read the figures

- Every amount is in **credits as the screens show them** (docs/MONEY.md).
- A cost or a yield in goods is turned into credits at the Exchange's usual price: materials 150, manpower 200,
  helium-3 600, exotics 700, rare earths 900, isotopes 1,200 a unit. Clout is counted at 600, the rate the game itself
  sells it for.
- **Net a year** is yield less upkeep, at full prosperity. A real world yields (50 + prosperity) hundredths of this, so
  a world at prosperity 55 pays about what the table says and a poor one two thirds of it.
- **Payback** is cost divided by net a year. **Return** is net a year as a share of cost, which is the game's nearest
  thing to return on invested capital.
- The houses' figures were measured by running the game: all 30 houses of *The Old Lion*, seed 5, at the start and
  after 30 years with nobody steering the player's house.

## 6. What the comparison shows

1. **Returns are far too high, and the wrong way up.** The middle real business returns about 10% and pays back in
   ten years. Here the cheapest things pay back in one to three years (a Deep Mine in 13 months at 88%; a Salvage
   Guild at 82%; a Patronage Office in under a year), and the dearest pay 2 to 6%. A player who works this out never
   builds anything large. In the real world the ordinary asset earns the ordinary return and the exceptional one is
   rare, risky or closed to most.
2. **Houses save four fifths of their income.** Real great houses spent nearly all of theirs, on the household, wages,
   upkeep, family and show, and many lived in debt. Here there is almost nothing that must be paid each year, so
   treasuries pile up to seven years of income and money stops mattering by the middle of a game.
3. **Nobody is paid to do the work.** A hull's crew costs nothing a year. A world's officials cost nothing. The only
   wages in the game are the 1 to 3 credits of the house's court and 200 for a council post. In the real world wages
   are the largest running cost of nearly everything, and that is what would soak up the surplus in point 2.
4. **Pay is nearly flat.** A cadet draws 100 and the most senior admiral 700: seven to one. A consul is paid no more
   than a praetor, the junior consul is paid the same as a quaestor, and the Admiral of the Reach is paid nothing. A
   steward and a spymaster are paid the same, however good or loyal. Real ladders run ten to one inside one household
   and hundreds or thousands to one from top to bottom.
5. **Prices of single acts are out of scale with incomes.** A physician's visit (600) is a middle house's whole
   yearly income at the start, and six years of a cadet's pay. The census fee (6,000) is nine years of it. A dowry can
   be nine years of it. These were set when the whole economy was counted in tens.
6. **A hull is cheap to keep and dear to a house.** Upkeep is 4 to 13% of the cost, which is the modern figure and
   reasonable. But four hulls take 60% of the starting house's income, because incomes are small against the price of
   everything a house owns.
7. **Borrowing is the one thing already in proportion**: 7 to 8% a year. But against ventures that return 30 to 80%
   it is free money, so the proportion does no work.
8. **Everything moves in steps of 100**, because the rules still reckon in hundreds (docs/MONEY.md, M1). A yield is
   300 or 400, never 350, and a retune cannot be fine while that holds.

## 7. What a retune would aim at

These are targets, and they are mine. They are meant to be argued with.

| Ratio | Now | Aim |
|---|---|---|
| Return on an ordinary building or venture | 20 to 110% | 6 to 12% a year: pays back in 8 to 15 years |
| A risky venture (war, spikes, salvage) | 3 to 82% | 12 to 20% in a good year, a loss in a bad one |
| A great work | 2 to 30% | 0 to 4%: built for standing and what it unlocks, not for coin |
| What a house spends of what it takes in | 20% | 80 to 95% in an ordinary year |
| Wages (household, officers, crews) as a share of spending | under 1% | 30 to 50% |
| A hull in commission, a year, with its crew paid | 4 to 13% of cost | 10 to 20%; a third of that laid up |
| A navy at peace as a share of spending | 60% at the start | 20 to 40% |
| Debt | 7 to 8% | 5 to 8%, and a house can carry two or three years' income |
| The most senior rank's pay to the most junior's | 7 to 1 | 20 to 1 |
| A great house's income to a servant's wage | about 300 to 1 | 2,000 to 1 and more |
| Somewhere to live (a seat or town house for a relative: not in the game yet) | none | 4 to 6 years of that person's income |

### Salaries, as the owner described them

- **Every post is paid**: the house's court, the council, a world's governor, a fleet's admiral and captains, the
  offices of the Reach.
- **Each post has a going rate** by its grade. A servant, an officer of the household, a councillor, a governor and
  an admiral are five different sums, a few times apart.
- **The head sets each person's pay** below, at or above the going rate. Below it, loyalty falls and the able leave.
  Above it, loyalty rises and it costs.
- **The very good ask for more.** Somebody whose skill is well above what the post asks names a price above the going
  rate. Refused, they serve resentfully, or go to a house that will pay.
- **Offices of the Reach pay by rank**, the consul most, out of the Reach's own levy and not the house's treasury.

## 8. Two ways to do it

1. **Retune inside the hundreds.** Change the costs, yields and prices in the content files and the constants. Quicker.
   But a return of 8% on a building that costs 1,200 is 96 a year, and the books cannot hold anything between 0 and
   100, so most of the targets above cannot be hit.
2. **Rewrite the rules in single credits and retune as it is done.** This is the large job that docs/MONEY.md put off
   because it would move the whole balance. If the whole balance is to be moved on purpose, that reason is gone. It
   would be done in stages, measuring all 30 houses after each one with the same run that made the table in section 1:
   incomes and what must be paid each year; then what things cost and return; then salaries; then the prices of single
   acts.

I recommend the second.

## 9. Questions for the owner

1. Are the targets in section 7 the right ones? In particular: should an ordinary investment pay back in about ten
   years, and should a house spend most of what it takes in?
2. Should crews and officials be paid every year, so that wages become the main running cost?
3. Should great works stop being investments (0 to 4%) and be built for standing and what they unlock?
4. The first or the second way in section 8?
5. Is there to be "somewhere to live": a seat or a town house a relative buys with their own purse?

econ --seeds 3` prints all of it again.

### Ranged returns

Everything that returns more than its kind is meant to has its **cost raised** until it returns that, at usual prices.
Nothing is made cheaper and no yield is changed. The kinds, and what each is meant to return a year (these are mine):

| Kind | Return a year at usual prices |
|---|---|
| Markets, ports, power (credits only) | 7% |
| Farming and manpower | 8% |
| Finance | 9% |
| Common extraction (materials), trade, services, clout | 10% |
| Shipping | 12% |
| The strategic goods: helium-3, rare earths, isotopes, exotics | 15% |
| Speculative (the Salvage Guild) | 20% |
| A great work that makes materials | 10% |
| A great work that brings up a strategic good | 12% |
| A great work for show | what it pays now, and never above 5% |
| War ventures, the Insurance House, the Commodity Warehouse | left alone: thin at peace, rich in a war or a spike |

### What it shows

1. **Ranged returns end the pile by themselves.** After 30 years the middle treasury is 2,900, about one year's
   income, where today it is 26,600, about five. The surplus goes into dearer buildings.
2. **They also slow the game.** By year 10 there are 62 buildings standing where today there are 87, and half the
   ventures. By year 30 incomes are less than half today's (2,400 against 5,300). Whether that is right is a question
   of pace: with these prices a house's first building takes several years to afford.
3. **No scheme of salaries tried is heavy enough to shape anything.** The heaviest is 18 to 20% of income at the
   start and 7 to 11% after 30 years, because incomes grow faster than what is paid. Nobody was ruined (two houses of
   90 in the red at the worst), and the navies are about a tenth smaller under the heavier ones.
4. **Flat rates fall behind.** A wage fixed per hull or per building shrinks beside an income that grows sevenfold.
   Pay that is to stay a third of spending has to follow what the thing earns (a share of a building's yield, a
   share of a hull's cost) and not be a fixed sum.
5. **Paying crews "as a navy pays them" does not empty the yards**: 94 hulls against 107. The yearly upkeep a hull
   already carries is the larger cost.

### What this cannot say

- The other houses decide what to build with rules written for today's prices. They were not taught the new ones.
- Nobody played the player's house: it took the first answer to every report and built nothing.
- One start, three seeds. The middle house's treasury moves by thousands between runs; the direction did not.

### What I would do next

1. Put the ranged returns into the game, with starting treasuries raised so that a first building is two or three
   years away and not seven. This is a real change to every game, so it waits on the owner.
2. Try salaries again as **shares**: staff at a fifth to a third of what a building or venture yields, a crew at a
   tenth of its hull's cost a year, and measure the same way.
3. Then the prices of single acts (section 4), against the new incomes.
